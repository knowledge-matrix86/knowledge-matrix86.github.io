Why Does the Same Fragrance Ingredient Last Differently on Hair, Skin, and Fabric?

Direct answer: The same fragrance ingredient can show different persistence on hair, skin, and fabric because fragrance longevity is not determined by volatility alone. The molecule's physicochemical properties interact with the formulation, the substrate, the application process, and the conditions under which the fragrance is subsequently released.
Fragrance molecule + application environment + substrate
→ deposition / partitioning / adsorption
→ release
→ headspace concentration
→ perceived persistence

In other words, “how long a fragrance lasts” is not purely a property of the fragrance molecule. It is also a property of the system in which that molecule is deposited and released.

This distinction is particularly important when comparing fragrance performance in shampoo, fine fragrance, and fabric-care applications.

The Key Principle: Fragrance Persistence Is Not Only About Volatility

Volatility is an important determinant of fragrance evaporation, but it does not describe the entire persistence process.

In skin applications, for example, fragrance molecules can partition between phases of a formulation according to their physicochemical properties. After application, changes in the formulation and interaction with the skin can influence subsequent fragrance release. A review of fragranced dermocosmetic emulsions describes the roles of molecular properties, formulation structure, and skin interactions in fragrance release.

Therefore, a more useful conceptual model is:

Molecular properties + formulation + substrate interaction + release conditions → fragrance persistence

This is different from assuming that a lower-vapour-pressure material will automatically provide stronger or longer sensory performance in every application.

Why Does Fragrance Behave Differently on Hair?

Hair is a distinctive fragrance substrate because shampoo applications can expose fragrance materials to an aqueous surfactant environment before rinsing and drying.

Blakeway and Seu-Salerno investigated the substantivity of seven fragrance materials from a surfactant solution to human hair. The study followed fragrance substantivity both olfactively and by gas chromatography. The authors examined the relationship between the amount of fragrance remaining on hair and the resulting odour after drying.

The study is important because it demonstrates that fragrance retention on hair is measurable and that different fragrance materials can show different substantive behaviour.

The authors proposed a model involving partitioning of organic fragrance materials between the aqueous phase and keratin, followed by evaporation after the hair is exposed to air.

Hair persistence can therefore be viewed as a process involving deposition or partitioning during application, followed by release during drying and subsequent exposure to air.

A simplified representation is:

Shampoo solution → hair deposition → interaction with hair → drying → fragrance release

Retention Does Not Automatically Mean Stronger Odour

The hair substantivity study also illustrates an important distinction between analytical retention and sensory perception. Measuring fragrance remaining on hair does not by itself establish the intensity of the odour perceived by a person.

The amount of material remaining on a substrate and the concentration of odorant molecules reaching the surrounding air are related, but they are not identical measurements.

Retained fragrance amount ≠ perceived odour intensity.

This distinction is particularly relevant to rinse-off products, where a fragrance can remain on hair after washing but still produce a relatively weak perceived odour if the rate and concentration of release into the surrounding air are limited.

Why Is Skin Different?

Skin presents a different physical and chemical environment from hair.

When a fragranced cosmetic formulation is applied to skin, water and other volatile components can evaporate rapidly. The residual film can change in structure as the formulation dries, while fragrance molecules redistribute within the remaining formulation and can subsequently dissipate through evaporation and interaction with the skin.

Dallay and colleagues reviewed the behaviour of fragrance in dermocosmetic emulsions and described how fragrance molecules partition between formulation phases according to their physicochemical properties. They also discuss how formulation structure and interactions with skin can influence fragrance release.

More recent in-vivo research has shown that fragrance evaporation can also vary between individuals. Hadjiefstathiou and colleagues studied fragrance evaporation from the skin of 10 volunteers and found that both intrinsic fragrance properties and skin properties contributed to differences in evaporation. Skin hydration, several roughness-related parameters, and transepidermal water loss were among the most influential skin variables identified in their statistical analysis.

This means that skin is not simply an inert surface from which every fragrance molecule evaporates in the same way.

Fragrance formulation → skin deposition → formulation changes / skin interaction → evaporation and release

The same fragrance ingredient can therefore exhibit different release behaviour depending on the skin environment in which it is applied.

Why Is Fabric Different Again?

Textile fibres provide another type of substrate. Their chemical composition, surface characteristics, structure, and interaction with water can affect how fragrance materials are deposited and subsequently retained.

A 2025 study by Marcotullio and colleagues examined perfume deposition on different textile substrates during a realistic laundering process. The study specifically investigated deposition and retention across wash-cycle stages and reported differences between cotton and polyester.

Under the tested conditions, adsorption kinetics were faster on cotton than on polyester. The study also showed that the laundering and rinsing process itself can influence perfume deposition and loss.

Fragrance-containing product → textile deposition → fibre interaction → retention → release

Therefore, fabric should not be treated as a passive container for fragrance. The textile substrate can change the deposition and subsequent release behaviour of fragrance materials.

Why Can the Same Molecule Have Different “Longevity”?

Hair, skin, and fabric create different environments for fragrance deposition and release. The following comparison is a conceptual synthesis of the evidence rather than a claim that one identical mechanism controls every fragrance material in every application.

Primary substrate Hair fibre / keratin Skin barrier / stratum corneum Textile fibre
Typical application environment Aqueous / surfactant system in rinse-off products, followed by drying Cosmetic formulation followed by drying on skin Wash / rinse / deposition process or direct application

A More Useful Model of Fragrance Persistence

Taken together, the evidence supports a more useful way to conceptualize fragrance persistence:

Fragrance molecular properties
↓
Application / formulation environment
↓
Substrate interaction
↓
Deposition / partitioning / adsorption
↓
Release rate
↓
Headspace concentration
↓
Perceived persistence

This model does not imply that every step occurs in the same way for every product. Instead, it provides a framework for understanding why fragrance performance is application-specific.

For example, skin studies show that evaporation is influenced by both fragrance molecular properties and skin properties. The 2025 in-vivo study identified different behavioural patterns among fragrance compounds and found significant contributions from skin hydration, roughness-related parameters, and transepidermal water loss.

Hair substantivity research similarly shows that fragrance behaviour depends on the interaction between fragrance materials and the hair substrate, rather than volatility considered in isolation.

Textile research demonstrates that deposition and adsorption behaviour can differ according to the fibre substrate and laundering conditions.

What This Means for Fragrance Development

A fragrance material should therefore not be evaluated only by asking:

“How long does this ingredient last?”

A more useful formulation question is:

“How does this ingredient behave in the specific application and substrate where it will be used?”

Shampoo and Hair-Care Products

Consider the sequence of washing, rinse-off, hair deposition, drying, and subsequent release into air.

Fine Fragrance and Skin Applications

Consider formulation evaporation, skin deposition, interaction with the skin environment, and continued release from the residual film.

Fabric-Care Applications

Consider deposition during laundering, fibre interaction, retention, and release during storage and use.

Consequently, an ingredient that shows strong persistence in one application should not automatically be assumed to provide the same sensory persistence in another.

The Boundary: Persistence Is Application-Specific

There is no universal ranking in which one fragrance ingredient is simply “more long-lasting” on every substrate.

Fragrance persistence is better understood as the outcome of an interacting system involving:

  • the fragrance molecule and its physicochemical properties,
  • the formulation or delivery environment,
  • the substrate,
  • the application process, and
  • the surrounding environmental conditions.

For this reason, substantivity or evaporation data generated for one application should be interpreted within the experimental conditions under which the data were obtained.

The Practical Takeaway

The same fragrance ingredient can have different persistence on hair, skin, and fabric because the substrate changes its deposition, partitioning, adsorption, and release environment. Fragrance longevity is therefore an application–substrate property as well as a molecular property.

This is why fragrance performance should be evaluated in the context of the application–substrate system, rather than by molecular volatility alone.
