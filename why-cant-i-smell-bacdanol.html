Why Can't I Smell Bacdanol? Specific Anosmia Explained

Short answer: Some people may be unusually insensitive to Bacdanol even when their general sense of smell is normal.

Scientific studies have specifically used Bacdanol in research on specific anosmia — a markedly reduced ability to detect a particular odorant despite otherwise normal olfactory function.

However, simply finding Bacdanol weak does not prove that you have specific anosmia. Concentration, smelling conditions, adaptation, and individual differences in odor sensitivity can also affect what you perceive.

What is Bacdanol?

Bacdanol is a synthetic fragrance material associated with a sandalwood odor profile. Its CAS number is 28219-61-6.

IFF, the supplier of Bacdanol®, describes it as a powerful sandalwood material with high diffusion and long-lasting fragrance performance.

This matters because an inability to perceive Bacdanol cannot simply be explained by saying that Bacdanol has little or no odor. Instead, perception can differ substantially between individuals.

Is specific anosmia to Bacdanol scientifically documented?

Yes.

A 2025 study published in Cortex investigated specific anosmia using four odorants: androstenone, benzyl salicylate, Bacdanol, and maltol.

Researchers initially screened 335 participants with an otherwise healthy sense of smell. Participants showing specific anosmia to at least one of the tested odorants were subsequently included in an olfactory-training experiment.

A total of 77 participants with at least one specific anosmia entered the two-month training phase.

The study therefore provides direct experimental evidence that Bacdanol can be an odorant for which large individual differences in detection occur.

This is not the first study involving Bacdanol

Earlier research also examined specific anosmia to Bacdanol.

A 2020 study tested 800 children and adolescents aged 5–14 years using Bacdanol, methyl salicylate, and 3-hydroxy-2-methyl-4-pyrone.

The researchers reported that the prevalence of specific anosmia to the tested odorants decreased from childhood to adolescence. They suggested that developmental changes in peripheral olfactory mechanisms may contribute to this pattern.

Together, these studies make Bacdanol particularly interesting because it is not merely a perfumery material discussed anecdotally by perfumers: it has also been used directly in controlled research on human odor detection.

Why might Bacdanol smell strong to one person and weak to another?

Human odor perception is not identical across individuals.

Possible explanations include:

  • Specific anosmia: unusually low sensitivity to Bacdanol itself.
  • Detection threshold differences: people can require different concentrations before an odor becomes detectable.
  • Olfactory adaptation: repeated or continuous exposure can temporarily reduce perceived intensity.
  • Smelling conditions: dilution, blotter testing, temperature, and surrounding odors can alter perception.
  • Individual olfactory biology: human odor perception varies substantially from person to person.

Does failing to smell Bacdanol mean I have specific anosmia?

No. Not necessarily.

Specific anosmia is a specific sensory phenomenon and should not be inferred from a single casual smelling test.

For example, if you repeatedly smell a material and its intensity gradually disappears, olfactory adaptation may be a better explanation.

Likewise, a highly diluted sample may simply fall below your individual detection threshold.

The scientifically defensible conclusion is therefore:

Specific anosmia to Bacdanol is documented in scientific research, but an individual who finds Bacdanol weak cannot determine the cause from that observation alone.

Can sensitivity to an odorant change with training?

Possibly.

The 2025 Cortex study examined whether repeated olfactory training could increase sensitivity to odorants for which participants showed specific anosmia.

After two months of training, participants became more sensitive to odorants they had previously been specifically anosmic to, while sensitivity to odorants already perceived at baseline did not show the same change.

The researchers also found evidence that at least some training effects may not be permanent.

This suggests that odor perception is not necessarily completely fixed, although the extent and persistence of training effects require further research.

A simple way to compare your perception

If you are experimenting with fragrance materials, avoid judging Bacdanol from a single sniff.

  1. Compare Bacdanol with several other sandalwood-type aroma materials.
  2. Use controlled dilutions rather than repeatedly smelling concentrated material.
  3. Use fresh blotters and allow time between evaluations.
  4. Repeat the comparison on different days.
  5. Compare your observations with those of other people under the same conditions.

These observations cannot diagnose specific anosmia, but they can help distinguish persistent individual sensitivity differences from temporary adaptation or testing conditions.

Key takeaway

If Bacdanol seems almost odorless to you while another person finds it powerful, the difference can be real.

Bacdanol has been directly used in scientific studies of specific anosmia, showing that substantial person-to-person differences in detection of this fragrance material can occur.

But specific anosmia is only one possible explanation. Properly controlled comparisons are needed before drawing conclusions about why a particular person perceives Bacdanol weakly.
